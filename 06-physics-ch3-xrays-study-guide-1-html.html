01 · The connected chapter pathway

Production → X-ray energy → Penetration → Attenuation → Radiograph → X-ray spectra → Uses

How everything connects: Energetic electrons hit a metal target and produce X-rays. The tube voltage (kV) controls X-ray energy and penetrating ability; mA and exposure time control the amount of radiation. As X-rays pass through tissues, their intensity decreases by attenuation. Different tissues absorb different amounts, producing contrast on the detector. X-ray spectra describe the wavelengths and characteristic peaks of the radiation.

02 · X-rays: definition & discovery

  • Discovery: X-rays were discovered in 1895 by the German physicist Wilhelm Roentgen.
  • Definition: X-rays are an extremely penetrating type of electromagnetic radiation.
  • Origin: Produced by bombarding a metal target such as copper or tungsten with energetic electrons, typically 50–100 keV (as stated in the slides).
  • Minimum continuous X-ray wavelength (λmin): Independent of the target composition; depends only on the tube voltage V.
Memorize: 1895 · Wilhelm Roentgen · electromagnetic radiation · metal target · λmin depends on tube voltage, not target composition.

03 · How do medical X-rays work?

  1. The patient is positioned with the body part of interest between an X-ray source and an X-ray detector.
  2. X-rays pass through the body and are absorbed in different amounts by different tissues.
  3. The absorption depends on radiological density, determined by both the material's density and atomic number (number of protons in the nucleus).
  4. Bones contain calcium with a higher atomic number than most other tissues; bones absorb X-rays readily.
  5. As a result, bony structures appear whiter than other tissues on the radiograph.
Cause → effect: Higher absorption by bone → fewer X-rays reach the detector behind the bone → greater contrast → bone appears white.

ليش العظم أبيض؟ لأنه يمتص X-rays أكثر من الأنسجة المحيطة.

04 · Production of X-rays

  1. Electrons are released from a hot element / hot cathode.
  2. They accelerate through a potential difference of approximately 1 kV to 1 MV toward a metal target anode.
  3. On impact, electrons are suddenly decelerated.
  4. Some electron kinetic energy converts to electromagnetic energy as X-rays.
Hot cathode → accelerated electrons → metal anode → sudden deceleration → X-rays
Efficiency: Less than 1% of supplied energy is converted into X-radiation during this process.

التسارع بين الكاثود والأنود، وبعدها التباطؤ المفاجئ عند الهدف، هو أساس توليد الأشعة.

05 · X-ray wavelength, frequency & energy

PropertyRange given in slides
Wavelength10 picometers to 10 nanometers
Frequency30 petahertz to 30 exahertz (3 × 1016 to 3 × 1019 Hz)
Photon energy145 eV to 124 keV
  • X-ray wavelengths are shorter than UV rays.
  • X-ray wavelengths are typically longer than gamma rays.
  • X-rays belong to the same electromagnetic radiation family as radio waves, microwaves, visible light and gamma rays.
Concept link: Higher photon energy ↔ higher frequency ↔ shorter wavelength. This follows the energy/wavelength equations used later in the slides.

Hard = high energy + short wavelength + high penetration. Soft = lower energy + longer wavelength.

09 · X-ray attenuation: the central laws

A. Inverse square law

In a vacuum, beam intensity decreases with the square of distance from the source.

I = I0 / r²

Expression shown in the slide. It represents the inverse-square relationship; the distance reference must be consistent when comparing physical intensities.

I ∝ 1 / r²

كلما زادت المسافة عن المصدر، تقل الشدة بحسب مربع المسافة.

B. Attenuation through material — Lambert law

Beam intensity decreases as X-ray photons interact with atoms in the material, through absorption and scattering.

−dI / I = μ · dx

μ = total linear attenuation coefficient; fraction absorbed or scattered per unit absorber thickness. It depends on the medium and X-ray energy.

C. Exponential attenuation

I = I0 e−μx
SymbolMeaningUnit in slides
I0Intensity of the incident beamW m−2
IIntensity after attenuation through materialW m−2
μLinear absorption / attenuation coefficientm−1
xDistance travelled through the materialm
Source wording note: One slide labels I as “reflected beam”; in the context of the transmitted-beam attenuation equation, I represents the remaining beam intensity after passing through the material. This note distinguishes the slide wording from the interpretation of its equation.

Half thickness: The thickness of material needed to reduce X-ray intensity to half its original value at a particular frequency.

X-ray intensity decays exponentially in absorbing material. More thickness x or larger μ → less transmitted intensity.

10 · Absorption example: model arm

The slide compares parallel beams passing through muscle and bone.

MaterialLinear absorption coefficient
Muscleμ = 0.20 cm−1
Boneμ = 12 cm−1

For the beam directed along line M: distance through muscle x = 8.0 cm.

I / I0 = e−μx = e−(0.20)(8.0) = e−1.6 ≈ 0.202

Result: About 20.2% of incident intensity remains after 8.0 cm of muscle in this model.

Unit check: μ in cm−1 must be paired with x in cm, so μx has no units.

11 · What controls attenuation?

The attenuation coefficient depends strongly on:

  • X-ray energy E
  • Density ρ
  • Atomic number Z
  • Atomic mass A
μ ∼ (ρ · Z⁴) / (A · E³)

This is the proportional relationship presented in the lecture.

Example atomDensity (slide)Z (slide)A (slide)
Oxygen O0.001 g/cm³8≈ 16
Iron Fe9 g/cm³27≈ 59
Cadmium Cd9 g/cm³48≈ 112
Lead Pb11 g/cm³82≈ 207

Values are reproduced as printed in the lecture, including its approximate example values.

Mass attenuation coefficient

Characterises how easily a material can be penetrated by X-rays. It is described as a variant of absorption cross-section defined per unit mass instead of per particle.

μm = μ / ρm
  • μ: linear attenuation coefficient
  • ρm: density of material
  • μm: mass attenuation coefficient
Distinguish: Linear attenuation coefficient (μ) versus mass attenuation coefficient (μm).

12 · Properties of X-rays

Study the complete lecture list:

  1. Can be absorbed by many materials.
  2. Can pass through other materials more or less unchanged.
  3. Cause fluorescence in rock salt, calcium compounds or uranium glass.
  4. Affect photographic plates.
  5. Cannot be refracted (n ≈ 1) — wording used in the lecture.
  6. Are unaffected by electric and magnetic fields.
  7. Discharge electrified bodies by ionising surrounding air.
  8. Can cause photoelectric emission.
  9. Are produced when high-energy electrons strike a metal target.
  10. Higher nucleon number of the target → greater intensity of X-rays produced.
  11. Are electromagnetic radiation similar to radio waves, microwaves, visible light and gamma rays.
  12. X-ray photons are highly energetic (the slide adds that they have enough energy “to break up”).
  13. Generally, higher energy → more X-rays pass through.
Source fidelity: “Cannot be refracted (n ≈ 1)” is the lecture's simplified statement; real X-rays can undergo very small refraction under suitable conditions. For lecture-specific recall, use the exact slide wording.
Common MCQ: “Which statement is not correct for X-rays?” → Can be refracted (according to this lecture's property list).

13 · X-ray spectra

The graph of X-ray intensity versus wavelength shows three key features:

  1. Continuous background: intensity varies smoothly with wavelength; it rises to a maximum and then decreases at greater wavelengths.
  2. Minimum wavelength (λmin): depends on tube voltage. Higher voltage → smaller minimum wavelength.
  3. Sharp intensity peaks: appear at characteristic wavelengths unaffected by changing tube voltage.
Remember the distinction: Voltage changes λmin, but the positions of the characteristic peaks depend on the target material.

14 · Minimum wavelength in the X-ray spectrum

For the limiting case in which the electron's entire kinetic energy is converted to one photon:

  1. Work done accelerating each electron through voltage V is eV.
  2. Maximum photon energy is hfmax = eV.
  3. Maximum frequency:
fmax = eV / h

Using c = fλ:

λmin = hc / eV
SymbolMeaning
hPlanck's constant
cSpeed of light
eElectron charge magnitude
VTube voltage / accelerating potential difference
fmaxMaximum frequency
λminMinimum wavelength
Exam relation: V ↑ → fmax ↑ and λmin ↓. λmin is independent of the metal target composition.

15 · Characteristic X-ray spectra

  1. Different target materials produce different wavelengths for the sharp peaks.
  2. Energetic electrons knock out inner-shell electrons from target atoms.
  3. The resulting inner-shell vacancies are refilled by electrons.
  4. During the transition, X-ray photons are emitted.
  5. The peak wavelengths define the target element's characteristic X-ray spectrum.
Inner-shell electron removed → vacancy refilled → characteristic X-ray photon emitted
Contrast: Continuous spectrum = smooth background; characteristic spectrum = sharp peaks specific to target material.

18 · Active recall — test yourself

Try answering before opening each item.

1. Who discovered X-rays, and when?

Wilhelm Roentgen, in 1895.

2. Why do bones appear white in a radiograph?

Bone calcium has relatively high atomic number and absorbs X-rays more strongly, producing contrast.

3. What does increasing kV change?

Increases X-ray energy and penetrating ability.

4. What do mA and exposure time change?

They increase the amount of X-rays produced.

5. What distinguishes hard from soft X-rays?

Hard X-rays have higher photon energies, shorter wavelengths and greater penetrating ability.

6. State the exponential attenuation law.

I = I₀e−μx.

7. What is half thickness?

Thickness of material reducing beam intensity to half the original value.

8. Which factors influence μ?

X-ray energy E, density ρ, atomic number Z and atomic mass A.

9. What is the relation between μm and μ?

μm = μ / ρm.

10. What happens to minimum wavelength when voltage rises?

Minimum wavelength decreases.

11. What causes characteristic peaks?

Inner-shell vacancies are refilled, emitting characteristic X-ray photons.

12. What are the three main uses at the chapter end?

Medicine, industry and X-ray crystallography.
